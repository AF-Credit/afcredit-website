An Auction Purchase With Tenants In Situ and a Lease in Dispute

The buyer had taken a freehold end-terrace in Preston at auction in August 2026 for £87,500, in her personal name. Auction purchases run to a fixed completion date and strict special conditions — miss it and the deposit goes with it.

The complication was on the title. The property was subject to a 25-year social housing lease, and the leaseholder was in significant arrears with a live rent dispute running. The seller had indicated the lease would be surrendered before completion, but an auction contract is not the place to rely on an indication. If the surrender did not happen in time, the buyer would still be obliged to complete — with the lease, the tenant and the dispute all still in place.

That produced a single, non-negotiable requirement: the lender had to fund the property exactly as it stood, encumbered, with no lease-surrender or vacant-possession condition attached to the offer. And it had to confirm that in writing, before any non-refundable fee was incurred.

This was not a theoretical concern. One specialist lender had already given indicative terms on the property as-is, then declined at application stage once its own legal review reached the lease. That is an expensive way to find out. A second application was running elsewhere at the same time, and it would spend the next fortnight going nowhere.

How We Funded the Auction Property Encumbered

The enquiry arrived at 10:24 in the morning. We asked two questions: what the rent dispute actually was, and what the leaseholder was paying against what the lease obliged them to pay. Both were answered within five minutes.

That was genuinely all we needed to form a view. The dispute was about arrears, not about the validity of the lease or the title. An arrears dispute affects income, and income was not our exit — a refinance was. The security was a freehold house in a known market, with the lease fully documented in the auction pack. At the LTV we were discussing, there was room for the lease to weigh on value and the loan still to sit comfortably.

Written terms were issued at 11:06. Forty-two minutes from first contact, with the lease position accepted explicitly and in writing, before the borrower had committed to a single non-refundable cost.

The facility was assessed on a desktop valuation, with no physical valuation required, at 75% LTV against a property value of £87,000. That supported a £65,250 gross loan with interest retained, delivering a net advance of £52,476.

We put the legal question where it belonged. We did not ask for the dispute to be resolved. We asked our solicitors to confirm two things. That our position as lender was protected. And that no liability from the dispute, the side letter or the management arrangements could pass to us. That is a question a lawyer can answer by reading the pack, which is exactly what the previous lender had used as grounds to withdraw. Our solicitors subsequently confirmed that our position was protected, with no liability inherited from the dispute, any side letter or the management arrangements.

We used dual representation. One firm acted for borrower and lender, covering the lender's legals, the borrower's financing legals and the purchase itself. On an auction deadline, removing a second firm from the chain removes a week of duplication.

On the Pricing

This facility priced at 1.25% per month, above the rate a straightforward case attracts. We would rather explain that than bury it.

AF Credit prices to complexity and timescale, not to loan size alone. A clean security with a clear title, a standard LTV and a comfortable deadline sits at the bottom of our range. This case carried an encumbered title, a live dispute, an auction clock and a legal review that had already defeated one lender. It took more underwriting and more legal work, and it was priced for what it was.

The important part is that the price, the fees and the deductions were all set out in writing at 11:06 on day one, and did not move afterwards. A cheap rate quoted by a lender who withdraws three weeks later costs considerably more than an honest one quoted in the first hour. With a longer lead time and a cleaner title, the pricing could have been lower.

Exit Once the Lease Position Resolves

The planned exit is a refinance onto longer-term finance once the lease position is resolved. Because the dispute related only to arrears and did not affect the validity of the lease or the title, and because the exit did not rely on the rental income in the short term, the position was acceptable.

For this buyer, the bridging facility provided a route to complete an auction purchase that other lenders would not fund, supported by a rapid written decision, a desktop valuation and dual representation that kept the transaction on the fixed deadline.

Key Underwriting Factors

Why This Was Lendable

  • The dispute was about arrears, not about the validity of the lease or the title — a distinction that changes the risk entirely.
  • Rental income was not the exit. A refinance was, so an income dispute did not undermine repayment.
  • 75% LTV against a desktop valuation of £87,000, leaving room for the lease to weigh on value.
  • Freehold house in an established market, with the lease fully documented in the auction pack rather than discovered later.
  • Lender's legal position confirmed as protected, with no liability inherited from the dispute or the management arrangements.
  • A borrower who disclosed the problem in her first email rather than hoping it would not surface.

Nothing here required a leap of faith. It required reading the legal pack and deciding which risks actually bore on repayment. The lease looked alarming on a checklist and turned out to be manageable on the facts — which is the difference between underwriting a case and scoring one.
