What a Rebridge Is, and Why This Borrower Needed One

A rebridge is a bridging loan used to refinance an existing bridging loan. Borrowers need one when the original facility reaches the end of its term before the planned exit has happened — a sale that has not completed, a refinance that has slipped, or, as here, a lender who agreed to refinance and then withdrew. It is not a sign the deal has failed. It buys the time to finish the exit properly instead of defaulting on a loan secured against the property. Our guide to refinancing a bridging loan covers how they are structured and what lenders look for.

Underwritten and funded by AF Credit as principal. This case study concerns a £128,250 unregulated bridging facility completed on 15 October 2026, following an internal property inspection and an in-house security assessment. Borrower details have been anonymised.

The Challenge

The borrower held a property in Newport with an existing bridging facility approaching its redemption date. The deadline was fixed and close. Miss it and the borrower would be in default on a loan secured against the property.

A refinance had already been arranged — or so it appeared. Another lender had taken the case on, progressed it as far as legals, and then withdrawn. The stated reason was the property's planning history. That history had not changed at any point during the month the case sat with them; it was visible from the first day. The borrower lost four weeks and arrived at AF Credit with the deadline far closer than when they started.

There was a further complication. A valuation report on the property already existed, but it had been prepared on the wrong instructions — commissioned for a different basis than the transaction actually required. It could not simply be relied upon, and re-instructing a fresh valuation would have consumed days the borrower did not have, and money they had already spent once.

The Planning Position

The planning history was genuinely messy, which is precisely why it had frightened off a lender working to a checklist.

  • A 2007 consent existed for student accommodation and a self-contained flat. The vendor had declared that works commenced, but the local authority could not confirm whether enough had been done to keep the permission live.
  • Several later applications to operate the property as a house in multiple occupation — at seven beds, then six — had been refused.
  • A further HMO application had been withdrawn shortly before our involvement.
  • The borrower had sensibly abandoned the HMO route and decided to return the property to a single five-bedroom dwelling, which required a fresh householder application and around £9,000 of works to finalise the layout.

In short: the property's lawful planning status was a grey area, and nobody could produce a document that made it black and white inside the deadline.

How We Underwrote It

We did not need the planning position resolved. We needed to be confident the loan was safe if it never was.

We instructed no valuation. Instead of commissioning a third report, we inspected the property internally ourselves. That told us what a desktop exercise could not: the actual condition, the standard of the works already done, and what remained to turn it back into a conventional five-bedroom house.

We ran our own numbers. Alongside the inspection we completed a residual calculation in-house: the end value of the property as a finished five-bedroom dwelling, less the outstanding works of roughly £9,000, less an allowance for the time and risk of getting there. That produced the value we were prepared to lend against on the day, rather than a figure that assumed the works were already done. It landed close to what the borrower needed, so the deal stood up without stretching the LTV to make it fit.

We lent against the building, not a planning outcome. The planning position was unresolved and we did not treat it as settled. We assessed the security as a conventional five-bedroom house — the use the borrower was reverting to, and the use the building is physically suited to — and asked whether the loan was safe on that basis alone. No part of the decision depended on an HMO consent being granted, or on the 2007 permission proving to be live. Had the planning never progressed, the security still supported the facility.

We dealt with the search risk rather than waiting on it. Local authority search timescales would have run past the deadline on their own. A personal local search was commissioned instead, backed by a search indemnity policy. To be precise about what that policy did: it covered the risk of something adverse being revealed by a search that had not been returned in full — it did not cure, regularise or insure the planning position itself, and it was never intended to. The planning remained the borrower's to resolve; the indemnity simply stopped an unpredictable council backlog dictating whether the deadline was met.

Key Underwriting Factors

What Made This Lendable When Another Lender Walked

  • Security assessed on its current lawful use as a standard dwelling, so no part of the decision rested on a planning outcome being granted.
  • Internal inspection carried out by AF Credit — direct evidence of condition, not a report written to the wrong brief.
  • Residual calculation completed in-house, working from end value less outstanding works, instead of leaning on a third-party figure.
  • Outstanding works of roughly £9,000 deducted from the view of value, not ignored.
  • 75% LTV against a conservative figure, leaving headroom if the planning position never improved.
  • A clear, deliverable exit: regularise the planning, then refinance onto a term facility.
  • A borrower who was open about the planning history in the first conversation, not hoping we would fail to look.

The planning history was not a detail anyone discovered late. It was there from the start, and it was the whole reason the case needed a lender who could think about it rather than score it. Pricing the risk and lending conservatively took hours. Waiting for certainty that was never going to arrive in time would have cost the borrower the property.
